00The one decision everything else hangs on

The firms that advertise “firearms merchant accounts” — PayKings, SecureGlobalPay, Corepay, Soar, Elite 2A — are ISOs, not processors. They underwrite you, place you with an acquiring bank that tolerates the industry, and then hand you a standard payment gateway. Your code talks to that gateway. It never talks to the ISO.

In practice you will be offered NMI, Authorize.Net, or USAePay. All three are ordinary, well-documented gateways with real client libraries and decades of production use.

Make this a condition of signingIf an ISO wants to put you on a proprietary gateway you have never heard of, walk away. Firearms merchants get dropped — by acquiring banks, after policy changes, sometimes with thirty days’ notice. On a standard gateway that is a credentials change. On a proprietary one it is a rewrite, executed under time pressure, while you cannot take money.

01The merchant account — start this week

Underwriting runs one to three weeks and can come back with conditions. Nothing in the code matters until this exists, so it is the critical path.

Have ready before applying

  • Copy of the FFL, plus SOT paperwork if suppressors will be sold
  • Business formation documents, EIN letter, and state sales tax permit
  • Three to six months of business bank statements
  • Processing history if any exists — even Square receipts from in‑store sales
  • Projected monthly volume and average ticket, stated honestly (understating to look safe causes account freezes when you exceed the cap)
  • A staging URL of the storefront with visible terms, refund policy, shipping policy, age gate, and the FFL transfer explanation — underwriters open the site and look for these
  • Driver’s license and SSN for the principal, for the personal guarantee

Negotiate these four, in writing

  • Rolling reserve. Expect 5–10% held for 180 days. That is working capital you cannot touch — model it before you commit to inventory.
  • Contract term and early termination fee. Three‑year terms with four‑figure ETFs are common. Push for one year or a waivable ETF.
  • Rate structure. Ask for interchange‑plus, not a flat blended rate. Expect roughly 3.5–5% plus $0.25–$0.35 per transaction.
  • Gateway ownership. Confirm the gateway account is in your business name and that you can re‑point it to a different acquirer. This is the portability clause that matters.
Billing descriptorSet it to something a customer recognizes on a statement — the shop name and phone number. A vague descriptor generates “I don’t recognize this charge” disputes, and in a high‑risk account your chargeback ratio is what keeps or loses you the merchant agreement.

02Keep PCI scope small

The single highest‑leverage technical decision: a raw card number must never reach your server or your logs. Use the gateway’s browser tokenizer, which renders the card inputs inside iframes it controls and hands your page back an opaque token.

Done this way you validate against SAQ A‑EP — a self‑assessment questionnaire. Post the card fields to your own backend and you fall into SAQ D, which means a formal audit, quarterly scans, and a compliance burden no small shop wants.

// Checkout page — NMI Collect.js. The card never touches your origin.
<script src="https://secure.nmi.com/token/Collect.js"
        data-tokenization-key="PUBLIC_TOKENIZATION_KEY"></script>

// Collect.js swaps these divs for iframes it owns:
<div id="ccnumber"></div>
<div id="ccexp"></div>
<div id="cvv"></div>

CollectJS.configure({
  callback: (res) => {
    // res.token is a one-time payment token. This is all you post.
    fetch('/api/checkout', {
      method: 'POST',
      headers: { 'Content-Type': 'application/json' },
      body: JSON.stringify({ orderId, paymentToken: res.token })
    })
  }
})
Three ways teams accidentally blow their PCI scopeLogging the full request body on the checkout endpoint. Capturing card fields in a session‑replay or analytics tool. Letting a support tool accept a card number over chat or email. Redact at the logger, exclude the checkout route from replay, and give staff a gateway‑hosted virtual terminal for phone orders.

03The order lifecycle

This is where a firearms site diverges from ordinary ecommerce, and where getting the sequence wrong creates a legal problem rather than a bug.

On a NICS denial the sale is terminated and the buyer’s money must be returned in full — you may not hold it or apply it to a future purchase. But the road from order to background check runs one to three weeks, and a card authorization typically expires in about seven days. So you cannot simply hold an auth until the transfer clears, and you should not capture at order time and hope.

Authorize at checkout. Capture when the rifle ships to the receiving FFL. Refund if the check comes back denied.

Rules the state machine has to enforce

  • Compliance gates authorization. Destination state rules, age, and destination‑FFL validation all run before the auth call. Never take an auth on an order you cannot legally ship.
  • Capture only what ships. Split shipments mean partial captures — ammunition going to the door and a rifle going to the FFL are two fulfilment events on one order.
  • Auth expiry is a real deadline. If the auth ages past roughly seven days without shipping, re‑authorize against the vaulted card rather than letting it lapse silently.
  • NICS denial refunds in full. No restocking fee, no store credit. Buyer’s remorse is different — there you may keep a disclosed restocking fee.
  • A “delayed” result is not a denial. It can resolve for up to three business days. Hold the order, do not refund.
  • Keep the chargeback evidence file. Tracking number, the receiving FFL’s acknowledgment of receipt, and the transfer completion record. That packet is what wins a dispute on a shipment the cardholder claims never arrived.

04The provider interface

Put the gateway behind a narrow interface on day one. This is not architectural purism — it is the specific insurance policy this industry needs. When the acquiring bank changes its mind about firearms, swapping providers should be a new class and a credentials change, not a checkout rewrite.

// payments/provider.ts — the only shape the rest of the app knows about

export type TxnStatus = 'approved' | 'declined' | 'error'

export interface Txn {
  id: string            // gateway transaction id — persist this
  status: TxnStatus
  authCode?: string
  avs?: string; cvv?: string
  raw: unknown          // full response, stored for dispute evidence
}

export interface PaymentProvider {
  authorize(i: AuthInput): Promise<Txn>
  capture(txnId: string, cents: number): Promise<Txn>
  voidAuth(txnId: string): Promise<Txn>
  refund(txnId: string, cents: number, reason: RefundReason): Promise<Txn>
  vault(paymentToken: string, c: Customer): Promise<string>
  chargeVaulted(vaultId: string, cents: number, o: MitOptions): Promise<Txn>
}

export type RefundReason =
  | 'nics_denied'      // mandatory, always full amount
  | 'buyer_cancelled'  // restocking fee may apply if disclosed
  | 'shipping_blocked' // destination state rejected post-order
  | 'goodwill'

The NMI implementation

// payments/nmi.ts — form-encoded in, url-encoded out

const ENDPOINT = 'https://secure.nmi.com/api/transact.php'

async function post(params: Record<string, string>) {
  const res = await fetch(ENDPOINT, {
    method: 'POST',
    headers: { 'Content-Type': 'application/x-www-form-urlencoded' },
    body: new URLSearchParams({ security_key: process.env.NMI_KEY!, ...params })
  })
  const r = Object.fromEntries(new URLSearchParams(await res.text()))
  // response: '1' approved · '2' declined · '3' gateway error
  return {
    id: r.transactionid,
    status: r.response === '1' ? 'approved'
          : r.response === '2' ? 'declined' : 'error',
    authCode: r.authcode, avs: r.avsresponse, cvv: r.cvvresponse, raw: r
  } as Txn
}

export const nmi: PaymentProvider = {
  authorize: (i) => post({
    type: 'auth',                   // 'auth', never 'sale' — capture comes later
    amount: (i.cents / 100).toFixed(2),
    payment_token: i.paymentToken,   // from Collect.js; no PAN here
    orderid: i.orderId,
    first_name: i.billing.firstName, last_name: i.billing.lastName,
    address1: i.billing.line1, city: i.billing.city,
    state: i.billing.state, zip: i.billing.zip,
    // ship-to is the receiving dealer, not the buyer — expected on every
    // firearm order, and the reason ship-to AVS is meaningless here
    shipping_address1: i.ffl.line1, shipping_city: i.ffl.city,
    shipping_state: i.ffl.state,     shipping_zip: i.ffl.zip,
    shipping_company: i.ffl.businessName
  }),
  capture:   (id, c) => post({ type: 'capture', transactionid: id, amount: (c/100).toFixed(2) }),
  voidAuth:  (id)    => post({ type: 'void',    transactionid: id }),
  refund:    (id, c) => post({ type: 'refund',  transactionid: id, amount: (c/100).toFixed(2) }),
  /* vault + chargeVaulted in section 06 */
}
Idempotency — this one bitesNMI has no idempotency header. A network timeout on authorize that you retry will authorize twice, and the customer sees two holds. Before every gateway call, insert a payment_attempts row keyed on (order_id, operation, attempt_key) with a unique constraint, and let the database reject the duplicate. Reconcile orphaned attempts against the gateway’s query API rather than assuming a timeout meant failure.

05Fraud controls, tuned for this industry

Firearms attract card fraud — high resale value, and the goods are anonymous once they leave. But the ordinary fraud playbook actively breaks here, so the rules need rethinking rather than copying.

Your best fraud control is the lawA stolen card cannot receive a rifle. The rifle goes to a licensed dealer who checks government photo ID and runs a background check against the person standing in front of them. That makes firearm orders structurally hard to defraud — and means the real card fraud on your site will land on optics, ammunition, and gear shipping to a residential door. Weight your rules accordingly: the $4,000 rifle is safer than the $1,200 scope.

Rules to configure

ControlSettingWhy this way
AVSRequire match on billing. Ignore ship‑to entirely.Ship‑to is an FFL and will never match the cardholder. A stock rule here declines every good order you take.
CVVRequired. Decline on mismatch, no exceptions.Cheapest signal available and the one card‑not‑present fraud most often fails.
3‑D Secure 2On for door‑shipped orders over $400.Shifts chargeback liability to the issuer. Worth the small conversion cost on exactly the orders that get disputed.
VelocityCap attempts per card, per IP, and per email hourly.Card testing hits firearms sites constantly because the tickets are large.
Manual reviewQueue door‑shipped orders over $800 from first‑time buyers.Cheap insurance where the structural FFL protection does not apply.
FFL validationCheck the destination against current ATF FFL eZ Check data.Compliance requirement and fraud control at once. Never accept a free‑text dealer address.

Watch the chargeback ratio like a business metric, not an accounting line. High‑risk acquirers terminate over sustained ratios above roughly 1%, and losing the account means the storefront cannot take money at all.

06Deposits, balances, and ACH

A custom build is not a single charge. It is a deposit that schedules the bench, then a balance weeks later — and the configurator is built around exactly that flow.

Stored credentials, flagged correctly

Charging the balance against a saved card is a merchant‑initiated transaction. Card networks require it be labelled as one and linked to the original. Skip this and issuers decline the balance charge at a much higher rate — the failure shows up weeks later, on your largest tickets, when the rifle is already built.

// Deposit — customer is present. This establishes the stored credential.
await post({
  type: 'sale', amount: '2090.00', payment_token: token,
  customer_vault: 'add_customer',
  initiated_by: 'customer',
  stored_credential_indicator: 'stored'   // establishing it
})

// Balance, 11 weeks later — customer is not present.
await post({
  type: 'sale', amount: '2090.00',
  customer_vault_id: vaultId,
  initiated_by: 'merchant',
  stored_credential_indicator: 'used',    // using it
  initial_transaction_id: depositTxnId       // links to the original
})

Give large builds a non‑card rail

On a $4,180 rifle, a 4.5% card fee is $188 of margin — on a build where the labour is already priced thin. Offer ACH or a wire for the balance and keep the card for the deposit.

  • Ask the ISO for ACH alongside the card account. Some bundle it; some do not, and a few ACH providers restrict firearms independently, so confirm in writing rather than assuming.
  • ACH is not instant and can return for insufficient funds days later. Do not ship on an ACH until it settles — typically three to five business days.
  • Until volume justifies automating it, handling large balances manually by invoice is entirely reasonable. Do not build a payments feature the shop uses twice a month.

Layaway is common in gun retail and rare online — a genuine differentiator. Mechanically it is the same stored‑credential pattern: a schedule of merchant‑initiated charges, with the firearm staying in the shop’s safe and logged in the bound book until the final payment clears and the transfer happens.

07Sales tax

One structural fact engineering needs, and one question for an accountant.

The structural fact: a firearm’s delivery point is the receiving FFL, not the buyer’s home. Tax situs generally follows the delivery address, so your tax engine must be fed the FFL’s address as ship‑to for the rifle, while accessories going to the buyer’s door are calculated against their home address. On a mixed cart those are two different destinations on one order.

Wire up Avalara or TaxJar rather than hand‑rolling rates — once distributor dropshipping puts you into dozens of states, economic nexus thresholds start triggering on their own.

Ask a CPA about excise taxesSeveral jurisdictions impose firearm‑ and ammunition‑specific excise taxes on top of sales tax — California added an 11% state excise on firearms and ammunition in 2024, and other states have followed or proposed similar. Who owes it in an interstate transfer depends on the state and on which dealer completes the transfer. This is a question for an accountant who works with FFLs, not a thing to infer from the statute.

08Before the first live charge

  • Merchant account approved, gateway credentials issued in the business’s own name
  • Sandbox transactions verified for every path — approve, decline, partial capture, void, full refund
  • Card fields confirmed to be gateway iframes; checkout request body redacted in logs; checkout route excluded from session replay
  • Compliance gate proven to run before authorization, with a blocked order never reaching the gateway
  • Auth expiry job in place, re‑authorizing rather than letting holds lapse
  • NICS‑denial refund path tested end to end, including the notification email to the buyer
  • Chargeback evidence packet assembling automatically — tracking, FFL receipt acknowledgment, transfer record
  • Billing descriptor set and verified on a real statement with a live test charge
  • Webhook or polling reconciliation so gateway state and order state cannot silently diverge
  • Refund and shipping policies published on the site — underwriters and issuers both look for them
  • Rolling reserve modelled into the cash flow, with the release schedule on the calendar
  • A written runbook for the day the processor drops the account, naming the backup ISO

Implementation guidance, not legal, tax, or compliance advice. Firearms retail obligations vary by state and municipality and change frequently — have an attorney who works with FFLs review the checkout flow and the refund policy before launch, and an accountant review the tax treatment. Gateway parameter names and API behaviour should be confirmed against current vendor documentation at build time.
